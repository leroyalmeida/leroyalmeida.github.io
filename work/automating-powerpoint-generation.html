I Shipped a Single Button

Automating PowerPoint Generation at Axiamatic

Problem

A strategic customer wouldn't adopt without automated PowerPoint reporting, right after we'd decided to move away from dashboards.

Solution

A single button with hardcoded templates and zero configuration, to test demand without pulling the product off strategy.

Timeline

3 weeks

Role

Senior Designer, worked on scope definition, stakeholder management, and interface design.

Axiamatic is an AI-native risk intelligence platform. A key customer asked for a way to turn their dashboards into a PowerPoint deck automatically.

The spec called for a configuration flow, a template library, and a drag-and-drop screen for mapping dashboards to slides. I shipped a single button.

This case study is about why cutting it down was harder than building the full version, and what it takes to design a first version meant to teach us something before it has to scale.

A customer ask that cut against our strategy

Our CTO had just announced a pivot: simplify dashboards, make the AI assistant the place users start, and stop competing with BI tools. Two weeks later, our most strategic customer said they wouldn't adopt without automated PowerPoint generation from their dashboards. We had just committed to moving away from dashboards, and now we were being asked to export them.

So I asked what we were really building. Was the value in the file itself, or in the AI pulling the data together and writing the summary? If it was the file, we were building a BI feature, the kind of thing the pivot told us to stop competing on. If it was the AI-written analysis, the feature moved our new direction forward. We were building it either way: the customer wouldn't adopt without the export, and a commitment to a key customer outweighed staying strictly on strategy. The question still set up the next one, which was how much of it to build.

Built for one customer, or for everyone?

The spec pulled two ways. Its title, "[redacted] Steering Committee Reporting," described one report for one customer's steering committee. Its interaction diagram showed screens for choosing templates, configuring dashboards, and mapping dashboards to slides, which is what you'd draw for a feature every customer could set up on their own.

Those lead to very different amounts of work. For one customer, we already knew which dashboards they used and what their deck needed to show. We could hardcode the templates around that and skip the configuration UI entirely, because there was nothing for the customer to choose. That's weeks of work.

A feature for every customer is a different problem. Every customer has different dashboards and wants different slides, so the product can't know in advance what goes where. Customers need screens to pick a template and their dashboards, and we need mapping logic that puts the right data on the right slide and handles the cases where they don't fit. Designing, building, and testing that takes months.

I talked the spec through with a few colleagues, and they saw the same tension. One doubted it would work beyond this customer, another suggested keeping it to a POC or MVP, and both asked whether in-product editing belonged in v1. That gave me a specific question for my PM: "Is this a platform feature or a customer-specific solution?"

We agreed on a hardcoded MVP for this one customer: one button, zero configuration. If it got used, we'd build the version for every customer later.

What I cut, and why

Even scoped to one customer, I could have built configuration, preview, and editing as if this were a platform feature. I argued for the smallest version that would tell us whether anyone wanted it, and I had to make the case for each cut.

Preview before download. The spec treated it as a given, but we had no evidence users needed to see the deck before downloading it. It meant more UI, more edge cases, and a longer timeline. Shipping without it tests that assumption for free: if users complain, we've learned they need it, and if they don't, we've saved weeks.

In-product editing. The spec listed it as a "future consideration," which is how scope creep gets in. I pushed to drop it entirely. Users can edit the deck in PowerPoint, which they already know and we don't have to build.

Discoverability. Teams usually want to promote a new feature. I argued for one button on one dashboard, with no announcement and no prompts from the AI assistant. If it works, we make it easier to find. If it doesn't, only a few people ever saw it, and we can remove it without anyone missing it.

What was left: a button that generates a four-slide PowerPoint deck from hardcoded templates and AI-written summaries.

Cutting was harder than building would have been. Preview felt obvious to stakeholders, so I had to explain that obvious isn't the same as validated. Editing was already in the spec, so removing it meant arguing with a written assumption. I also had to say exactly what we were giving up, how we'd know if I was wrong, and keep pushing back on "while we're at it," which is how small projects become big ones.

How we'll know if it worked

The MVP is scoped and designed. Now we see if the bet pays off.

It works if the customer generates their weekly deck on their own, preparing it takes less than 15 minutes, and they go four consecutive weeks without needing help from our support team.

It fails if they give up after two tries, if each deck needs 30+ minutes of editing, or if people go back to building decks by hand.

Either way, we learn what the version for every customer should be. Do users want all four slides or a subset? Do they edit the text more than the visuals? What do they add by hand after the deck is generated?

The version I'm not building yet

Designing a small first step means knowing the likely second one, so I've already explored the version for every customer. The hard problem is mapping: how does a customer say which dashboard feeds which slide, when every customer has different dashboards and wants different slides?

Approach How it works Trade-off
Template-driven Each template defines the dashboard types it needs, and users pick from compatible dashboards Simple for users but rigid for us: we'd have to maintain the list of which templates work with which dashboards
AI-assisted Users pick dashboards and templates independently, then the AI proposes a mapping for them to review Flexible and in line with our product direction, but complex to build and only as good as the AI's suggestions
Manual mapping Users drag and drop dashboards onto specific slides Full control but a lot of work for users, and it ignores the AI that's supposed to set us apart

I'd recommend AI-assisted mapping, since it's the only option that builds on our move toward an AI assistant instead of working against it. That recommendation stays on the shelf until we know the first version gets used.

Key learnings

I started out skeptical this should exist. Building a dashboard export right after moving away from dashboards felt like backsliding.

I learned that a feature can go against our stated direction and still be the right thing to build, because it unblocks a critical customer. The work was finding a version that served the customer without pulling the product off course.

Here, that version was very small: no configuration, no preview, no editing, no promotion. It was just enough to find out whether the idea is worth building out, and to let the results decide what comes next.
