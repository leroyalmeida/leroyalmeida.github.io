I Shipped a Single Button

Automating PowerPoint Generation at Axiamatic

On paper, this project was a configuration flow, a template library and drag-and-drop mapping. Instead, I shipped a single button.

This case study is about why that was the harder call, and what it takes to design for learning instead of scale.

A customer ask that cut against our strategy

Our CTO had just announced a pivot: simplify dashboards, make AI the primary entry point, stop competing with BI tools. Two weeks later, our most strategic customer said they wouldn't adopt without automated PowerPoint generation from dashboards.

I raised the question that mattered: were we building for the deliverable (a PowerPoint file) or for the generation (AI reasoning and synthesis)? The first is traditional BI tooling. The second advances our direction. The answer was clear: this customer wouldn't adopt without it, and customer commitments override strategic purity.

Platform feature or one-customer MVP?

The product doc pulled two ways. The title said "[redacted] Steering Committee Reporting," singular and specific. The interaction diagram showed template selection, dashboard configuration and mapping interfaces: plural, generic, platform-shaped.

A customer-specific solution means hardcoded templates, no configuration UI, and weeks of work. A platform feature means selection interfaces, mapping logic, and months.

I talked the spec through with a few colleagues, and they saw the same tension. One questioned whether it would scale beyond this customer, another suggested a POC/MVP, and both asked whether in-product editing belonged in v1. That let me ask my PM something specific: "Is this a platform feature or a customer-specific solution?"

We aligned on a hardcoded MVP for this one customer. One button, zero configuration. If it validated the pattern, we'd generalize later.

What I cut, and why

Even scoped to one customer, I could have built configuration, preview and editing as if for a platform. I argued for the minimum that would tell us whether anyone wanted this at all, and had to make the case for each cut.

Preview before download. The spec treated it as a given, but we had no evidence users wanted it. It meant more UI, more edge cases and a longer timeline. Shipping without it tests the same assumption for free: if users complain, we've learned something; if not, we've saved weeks.

In-product editing. The spec listed it as a "future consideration," which is how scope creep sneaks in. I pushed to drop it entirely. Users can edit in PowerPoint, which they already know and we don't have to build.

Discoverability. The instinct is to promote new features. I argued for the opposite: one button on one dashboard, no promotion, no AI assistant suggestions. If it works, we scale discoverability. If it doesn't, the failure stays cheap.

What remained: a button that generates a four-slide PowerPoint deck from hardcoded templates and AI-written summaries.

This was the harder path. Every cut was a conversation: explaining that obvious isn't the same as validated, pushing back on assumptions already written into the spec, and going against the team's launch instincts. It meant being precise about what we were trading away and how we'd know if we were wrong, and resisting "while we're at it," which is how small projects become big ones.

How we'll know if it worked

The MVP is scoped and designed. Now we see if the bet pays off.

What success looks like: The customer generates their weekly deck on their own. Prep time drops below 15 minutes, with zero support involvement for four consecutive weeks.

What failure looks like: Abandonment after two attempts. Decks that need 30+ minutes of editing. Users going back to building decks by hand.

What we're learning either way: Do users want all four slides or a subset? Do they edit text more than visuals? What do they add by hand after generation? Each answer shapes the generalized version, if we build one.

The version I'm not building yet

Designing a minimal first step means knowing the likely second one, so I've already explored generalizing it. The core challenge is mapping: how do customers say which dashboards feed which slides when both sides are flexible?

Approach Mechanism Trade-off
Template-driven Templates define required dashboard types upfront, and users pick from compatible options Simple for users but rigid for us, requiring a maintained taxonomy of template-to-dashboard relationships
AI-assisted Users select dashboards and templates independently, then the AI proposes a mapping for review Flexible and aligned with our product direction, but complex to build and dependent on AI quality
Manual mapping Users drag and drop dashboards onto specific slides Maximum control but high cognitive load, and doesn't use the AI that's supposed to differentiate us

I'd recommend AI-assisted mapping. It's the only approach that advances our assistant-first bet instead of contradicting it. That recommendation stays on the shelf until the core pattern is validated.

Key learnings

I started out skeptical this should exist. Building dashboard export right after moving away from dashboards felt like backsliding.

What I learned is that strategic alignment isn't binary. This feature contradicts our stated direction and unblocks a critical customer. Both are true. The skill is holding that tension long enough to find the version that serves the customer without derailing the strategy.

Here, that version was very small: no configuration, no preview, no editing, no promotion. Just enough to learn whether the pattern has legs, and to let the results decide what comes next.
