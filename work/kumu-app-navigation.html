Kumu App Navigation Redesign

Redesigning how new and existing users navigate and find key features and experiences in the app

Problem

How can we make the app easier to navigate for new and existing users?

Solution

Restructured the information architecture using familiar navigation patterns

Impact

Increased usage for Wallet, Profile, Missions, Watchlist, and Klips

Role

Lead designer, worked on IA, interface design, prototyping, and usability tests

At the time, Kumu was the largest social entertainment app in the Philippines: 10+ million users (82% Millennial and GenZ), 60 million visits a month, and over an hour a day per user, supporting thousands of income-earning Creators in 50+ countries.

New users came from social media ads and celebrity recommendations. They knew Kumu was a livestreaming app but not what to use it for, so onboarding them was one of our biggest challenges.

Why confused users cost money

Users who couldn't find Wallet, Missions, or Notifications were less likely to spend or build habits that drive retention. With Kumu spending heavily on acquisition, every churned user was wasted marketing money.

Our hypothesis: a better information architecture would raise feature adoption and reduce new user churn by making the app's value clear from the first session.

"How might we make the app easier to navigate for both new and existing users?"

What research and data told us

New users want to establish a mental model of the app

“Confusion” kept coming up in app reviews. UX research found new users felt the homepage was overwhelming and spent hours scrolling and joining streams to figure Kumu out. Analytics showed the same thing: new users' first move was clicking around to explore.

“Daming kailangan aralin pag new users. Pumapasok ako sa streams para matuto. Iniexplain lang niya kung paano gamitin. Masungit din siya so hindi ko po makausap ng maayos.”
(Translation: You need to learn a lot as a new user. I enter streams to learn. They explain how to use it. But they’re grumpy so you can’t talk to them properly.)

Kumu's app information architecture was poor

Campaigns, Missions, and Leaderboard each appeared in more than one of these menus, and our onboarding tour left the jargon unexplained. Users asked what Following, Hot, Multi, Klips, and Campaigns meant, and whether the Missions in the Profile menu was different from the one in the side menu.

The sheer number of features buried the value proposition. We needed to fix core navigation and steer users toward key jobs-to-be-done.

“Information architecture is the way that we arrange the parts of something to make it understandable.”
— Abby Covert, How to Make Sense of Any Mess

Notifications are a critical part of the Kumu experience

Notifications usage went up after we released the “User Followed You” notification, even though the page was still buried in the side menu. Fewer than 25% of users opened the side menu, but 50% of those who did clicked Notifications, the highest click-through rate of any item.

Design strategy

  • Remove redundant elements. Streamline the menus to eliminate duplicate paths.
  • Bring key features forward. Put critical features, like Notifications, in the main navigation.
  • Adopt familiar patterns. Use navigation conventions users already understand.

Five explorations, one pick

Kumu's Chief Product Officer drafted an initial information architecture, and over the next few days I developed four more explorations so we had real options to compare.

Evaluating the tradeoffs

I scored each one against three criteria from the research: familiarity (does it use patterns users know?), simplification (does it cut redundant entry points?), and feature elevation (does it surface Notifications?).

Exploration Strengths Weaknesses Recommendation
A Elevates Notifications to main navigation Still has separate Side Menu and Profile Menu; doesn't consolidate redundant paths Didn't solve core IA problem
B Consolidated menus well; Notifications visible in header Notifications in top bar is less familiar; users may miss it Less intuitive notification access
C Familiar pattern (Instagram-like); consolidates menus; elevates Notifications to main nav Requires users to learn new Profile location via avatar Recommended
D Surfaces many features directly in navigation 6-tab navigation is crowded; "Kumunity" and "Activity" labels unfamiliar to users Too complex for new users

I recommended Exploration C, the only one that met all three. It mirrored Instagram, Twitter, and LinkedIn, which our users already knew, and it fit the CPO's strategic direction, so we moved on to testing.

Testing before a big switch

This was a massive change built on an educated guess, so with the UX research team we tested whether it worked without breaking existing flows. Task completion rates:

  • Accessing Profile: 93.75%
  • Accessing Notifications: 93.75%
  • Accessing Notification Settings: 100%
  • Accessing My Watchlist: 66.75%
  • Adding FB friends: 81.25%
  • Sharing profile link: 68.75%
  • Scanning QR code: 87.5%
  • Editing my address: 81.25%
"All of us are used to seeing notifications here [Menu Tab]. It makes me want to see if I’m gaining more followers, are my friends livestreaming? Notifications won’t get buried anymore."
— Participant #2
"The notifications tab at the bottom navigation is very helpful. Before I had difficulty in seeing my notifications because I had to access the Menu and sometimes the profile page."
— Participant #7

Accepting two weaker results

I judged the lower scores on Watchlist (66.75%) and profile link sharing (68.75%) acceptable for launch. Watchlist was mostly used by power users who would adapt, and link sharing was an edge case. The primary flows all cleared our success threshold.

We shipped in two phases: on July 1, a Side Menu that consolidated entry points from both old menus; on July 20, Notifications replaced the Profile Menu in the main navigation.

Key learnings

  • Moving forward under ambiguity: Our data wasn't perfect, but doing nothing cost more than testing a well-reasoned hypothesis, and testing backed it with 93%+ completion on core tasks.
  • Design as a research tool: With UX Research's backlog full, concrete explorations got stakeholders aligned faster and gave us something to test.
  • Behavioral data over stated preferences: Users never asked for Notifications, but its 50% click-through from the side menu showed demand that better placement could unlock.
  • Strategic prioritization in testing: Not every task needed 90%+ completion, only the core ones.
  • Phased rollout as risk management: Shipping the side menu change two weeks before the navigation bar change let us isolate variables before the higher-stakes update.
  • Knowing when to ship: With more time, I'd have improved Watchlist discoverability, but waiting would have delayed the core wins for millions of users.
